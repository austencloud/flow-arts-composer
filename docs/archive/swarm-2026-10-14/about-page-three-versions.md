# About Page: Three Versions Compared

## VERSION 1: Original (AI-written, website copy)

### What Is TKA

The Kinetic Alphabet brings something novel to flow arts. Previous attempts to document flow arts patterns created valuable knowledge collections. TKA builds on that foundation.

Flow arts needs a way to describe patterns that expands beyond videos across the Internet. Sheet music tells musicians what notes to play. Actors memorize their lines so they can act. TKA works the same way: it documents the physical movements so you can reliably reproduce them later.

### How It Works

Every beat of movement becomes a pictograph showing where your hands are and how they move on a grid. The visual approach means you can read a sequence immediately without memorizing terminology. Positions describe where your hands are relative to each other: across from each other, at the same point, forming a right angle.

Pictographs also capture motion types, direction, and rotation. The letter system is optional, but useful when you need to reference a single beat in a specific sequence during choreography and rehearsal.

### Why This Matters

Flow arts is a very young art form. Many practitioners find it hard to collaborate with others due to physical distance or foundational differences in technique. Especially in the US, where TKA got its start, flow arts is a heavily solo-influenced art form.

The Kinetic Alphabet does not rely on English terminology, instead embracing symbols and pictures for its communication, making it shareable across languages and distances. It exists in both physical form (pen and paper) and digital form.

With documented patterns, choreographers can build on each other's work instead of recreating from scratch. You can archive your own material and revisit it months later.

### Who It's For

The Kinetic Alphabet is designed for flow arts teachers, choreographers, and spinners. For beginners and veterans alike, TKA bridges theory and performance. It exists to make synchronized group choreography more achievable, and it is also a tool for self-directed progression.

By tracking your individual parts, it makes choreography accessible even if you struggle with memory and executive function. It is split into multiple levels of increasing complexity and density, so you can learn at your own pace.

For those who value theatrical performance more than technical patterns, TKA handles the groundwork so you can focus on expression. Once you internalize its framework, it can be a foundation for expressive creativity.

### The Vision

TKA changes how you teach. Instead of demonstrating repeatedly, show them the pictograph. Share notated sequences with students anywhere in the world. The visual system works across languages.

Once you internalize the pictographs and vocabulary, you gain building blocks for thinking about movement.

TKA breaks prop movement into building blocks. Once you understand how pictographs work, you can create your own sequences with them and call them yours. The letter system shows you what you've already browsed and what's still unbrowseed, giving you a fountain of novelty to pull from.

The goal is greater collaboration and more ambitious live performances. The Kinetic Alphabet has already gained momentum. The movement is growing.

Right now, flow arts knowledge lives mostly in videos scattered across the internet. Sheet music tells musicians what to play. Scripts tell actors what to say. TKA does that for prop movement: write it down, read it back, reproduce it later.

TKA uses symbols and pictures instead of English terminology, so it works across languages. It works on paper and on screen.

When patterns are written down, choreographers can share and build on each other's work. You can also archive your own material and actually find it again months later.

Teachers, choreographers, spinners. TKA is meant to make synchronized group choreography more realistic, but it's also useful for solo practice.

## VERSION 3: Grant Application

### The Problem

Flow arts is a performance discipline practiced worldwide, but it has no standard notation system. Practitioners spin staffs, fans, clubs, and other props in patterns that can be as complex as any musical composition, but the knowledge exists almost entirely in video and oral tradition. There is no written form.

This creates concrete problems. Choreographers cannot write down what they want performed. Teachers must demonstrate every movement in person. Collaboration between geographically separated performers requires video calls and screen recordings. Performers who develop original material have no way to document it except to film themselves, and no way to search or index what they've created.

Several theoretical frameworks have emerged over the past two decades. The Vulcan Tech Gospel (VTG), developed by Noel Yee and Jordan Campbell in Oakland, CA, classifies timing and direction relationships between two spinning props. Charlie Cushing's 9-Square Theory quantizes the space around the performer's body into a navigable grid. Lorq Nichols' P.H.A.T. Chart catalogs 64 pattern variations in a visual matrix. Cushing and Ben Drexler developed QFT Notation, a precise but highly technical formula for recording any movement.

These systems are valuable and TKA builds on their ideas. Where they focus on classification and analysis, TKA focuses on a different problem: giving people a way to write movement down and share it. For comparison, a QFT formula looks like this: `a,b(h(+/-x+/-y+/-z)h'){Class}a',b'`. It is precise. TKA's equivalent is a pictograph you can draw on a napkin, or a word like "CAKE" you can text to a collaborator.

### What TKA Is

The Kinetic Alphabet (TKA) is a notation system that encodes prop movement as pictographs and letters. Each beat of a performance is represented as a pictograph: a grid-based diagram showing hand positions, prop orientations, motion types, direction, and rotation. Sequences of pictographs form words, and words spell out choreography that can be read, written, spoken, and reproduced.

The system is visual-first. A pictograph can be read without terminology training. Positions are geometric: hands at opposite points (alpha), the same point (beta), or at a right angle (gamma). Motion types are drawn, not described.

Letters are assigned to each distinct hand-path combination within a position. The current alphabet contains over 100 letters across six types. A sequence of movements becomes a word. "CAKE" is not a metaphor. It is four specific beats of movement, each unambiguous, each reproducible.

The notation does not depend on English or any natural language. It uses symbols, spatial diagrams, and single-character labels. This makes it transferable across linguistic and cultural boundaries without translation.

### The Design: Leveled Complexity

TKA is structured as a progressive system with nine levels of increasing complexity:

- **Levels 1-3** introduce turns (zero rotation, whole turns, half turns and float), building foundational movement vocabulary on a four-point grid
- **Level 4** completes prop orientation with the four interradial values and the quarter turns that reach them (eight orientations, cardinal and interradial)
- **Level 5** expands to an eight-point grid, adding diagonal positions
- **Level 6** adds a center point (nine-point grid), completing the single grid
- **Level 7** introduces conjoined grids (dual performers sharing a junction point)
- **Levels 8-9** extend into three dimensions

This structure means a beginner at Level 1 works with 6 possible single-hand motions from any grid point. By Level 5, that number grows to 212. The ceiling (all levels, quarter-turn granularity) reaches 237 distinct single-hand motions per grid point. The combinatorial space is large enough to sustain years of practice without repetition, and every motion within it is documented and indexable.

### Accessibility

TKA addresses specific accessibility barriers in flow arts education:

**Cognitive accessibility.** Many performers struggle with working memory and executive function demands of learning choreography by demonstration. TKA externalizes the memory requirement. The sequence exists on paper or screen. The performer references it rather than holding it in working memory. This parallels how sheet music supports musicians with memory difficulties, but no equivalent has existed for movement arts until now.

**Geographic accessibility.** A teacher in Portland can send a notated sequence to a student in Berlin. The student reads the pictographs and reproduces the movement. This does not replace in-person instruction for technique, but it eliminates the requirement that choreographic content be transmitted in person or by video.

**Linguistic accessibility.** The pictograph system is non-verbal. Positions are spatial relationships on a grid, not English words. Motion types are drawn arcs and arrows, not described in prose. A Japanese-speaking performer and an English-speaking performer read the same pictograph identically.

**Progressive complexity.** The level system means no learner confronts the full complexity of the system at once. Level 1 has six motions. Level 2 has thirty. The learner decides when to advance. This self-paced structure accommodates different learning speeds without requiring separate curricula.

### Prior Art and Differentiation

| Capability | VTG | 9-Square | QFT Notation | Shape Matrix | TKA |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Classify movement | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Predict valid transitions | Yes | Yes | Partially | No | Yes |
| Write on paper | No | No | Partially | No | Yes |
| Read without training | No | No | No | No | Yes |
| Speak aloud | No | No | No | No | Yes |
| Language-independent | Partially | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Enumerate full motion space | No | No | No | No | Yes |

TKA is not a replacement for these systems. VTG's timing/direction classification data is preserved within TKA's data model. The grid system shares conceptual DNA with 9-Square Theory. TKA's contribution is translating the analytical power of these frameworks into a communicable form.

### Current State

Flow Arts Composer is the digital implementation: a free, browser-based application for creating, browsing, and sharing notated sequences. It includes:

- A sequence builder for composing choreography beat by beat
- A constrained generator that produces valid sequences from the letter system
- A browsable library of existing sequences
- Animated playback showing the notation performed in real time
- Export and sharing tools for distributing sequences

The system also works on paper. The pictographs are designed to be hand-drawn. The letters are single characters. A choreographer with a pen and a printed grid template can notate a sequence without any software.

### What This Enables

Documented movement creates infrastructure that oral and video tradition cannot. Specifically:

- **Archival.** Original choreography can be stored, indexed, and retrieved. A performer's body of work becomes searchable.
- **Attribution.** When sequences are written down and shared, authorship is traceable. Originality is verifiable against the existing corpus.
- **Curriculum development.** Teaching progressions can be standardized, shared between instructors, and refined over time. The level system provides a built-in pedagogical scaffold.
- **Cross-group collaboration.** Multiple performers or groups can rehearse from the same notation independently, then combine. This is standard practice in music and theater. It has not been possible in flow arts.
- **Compositional analysis.** Written notation makes it possible to study choreographic structure the way musicologists study compositions: identifying motifs, variations, repetitions, and transformations.

## COMPARATIVE ANALYSIS

### Sentence-Level Patterns

| Pattern | V1 (Original) | V2 (Fire Jam) | V3 (Grant) |
|---------|---------------|---------------|------------|
| Avg sentence length | ~22 words | ~14 words | ~20 words |
| Shortest sentence | 6 words | 3 words | 5 words |
| Longest sentence | 38 words | 30 words | 35 words |
| Sentence variety | Low (uniform medium) | High (punchy + flowing) | Medium (formal but varied) |
| Passive voice | Moderate | Rare | Moderate (appropriate) |
| "You" direct address | Frequent | Frequent | Occasional |

### Tone

| Dimension | V1 | V2 | V3 |
|-----------|----|----|-----|
| Who's talking | A brochure | A person at a jam | A grant writer |
| Confidence level | Overselling | Casual confidence | Evidence-backed |
| Self-awareness | None (promo mode) | High (knows its audience) | Institutional (appropriate) |
| Specificity | Medium (vague benefits) | High (concrete actions) | Very high (numbers, names, tables) |

### What Each Version Does Well

**V1 (Original):**
- The sheet music / scripts analogy is strong (kept in all three)
- Covers all the right topics
- Mentions executive function (important, often forgotten)

**V2 (Fire Jam):**
- Sounds like a human being
- "Learn the notation, then forget about it while you perform" is the best single line across all three
- Short sentences create momentum
- Doesn't oversell. "TKA tries to take it further" is honest
- Respects the reader's time

**V3 (Grant):**
- Names prior art specifically (VTG, 9-Square, QFT, P.H.A.T. Chart) with creators credited
- Provides hard numbers (6 motions at L1, 129 at L5, 237 at ceiling)
- Differentiates clearly with a comparison table
- Frames accessibility as a structural argument, not a feature bullet
- Addresses archival, attribution, curriculum, collaboration, and analysis as distinct outcomes
- Does not claim "the movement is growing" — lets the evidence speak

### What Each Version Does Poorly

**V1 (Original):**
- "Brings something novel" — vague
- "Previous attempts... created valuable knowledge collections" — patronizing and unnamed
- "Builds on that foundation" — what foundation? Which work? Says nothing
- "For beginners and veterans alike" — dead AI pattern
- "Bridges theory and performance" — meaningless marketing phrase
- "Self-directed progression" — corporate HR language
- "Building blocks" repeated twice, "Once you" opens two paragraphs
- "The movement is growing" — self-congratulatory with no evidence
- "Fountain of novelty" — trying too hard
- "Unbrowseed" — typo

**V2 (Fire Jam):**
- Too casual for institutional audiences (funders, educators, arts councils)
- No evidence, no numbers, no citations
- "Other people have documented patterns before" — a grant reviewer would ask "who?"
- Doesn't explain *why* it works across languages (just asserts it)
- The level system is mentioned but not explained
- No sense of scale (how big is the alphabet? how many motions?)
- "That's fine" — too conversational for some contexts

**V3 (Grant):**
- Would bore a spinner at a jam in three seconds flat
- Nobody at a festival wants to read a comparison table
- Formal tone creates distance — doesn't feel like a community project
- Doesn't capture the *feeling* of using TKA ("once the system clicks, you start thinking in it")
- Missing the human voice entirely — you can't tell who made this or why they care
- The accessibility section is strong but reads clinical
- No mention of the emotional payoff (performance, expression, creativity)

### The Core Insight

Each version fails where the others succeed:

| Need | Best Version |
|------|-------------|
| Make someone *want* to try TKA | V2 |
| Convince a funder to support TKA | V3 |
| Exist on a website without embarrassing you | V2 |
| Win an arts grant | V3 |
| Explain TKA to your mom | V2 |
| Explain TKA to a university department | V3 |
| Explain TKA to another spinner | V2 |

V1 doesn't win any category. It sits in a middle zone: too formal to be warm, too vague to be persuasive, too promotional to be trusted. It has the structure of grant writing with the specificity of casual conversation, which means it serves neither audience.

The real answer is: you need both V2 and V3, deployed to different audiences. The website gets V2. Grant applications get V3. They shouldn't try to be the same document.
