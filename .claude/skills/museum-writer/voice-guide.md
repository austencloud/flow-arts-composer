# Museum Voice Guide — Character Sheets

## K: The Full Character

**Born:** ~1968. Male. Likely autistic (obsessive system-building, pattern recognition, structural thinking, consistent humor across modalities, precise rather than dramatic action).

**Core trait:** K sees what things look like before he understands what they mean. This is his cognitive fingerprint — it appears in both voices because it's how his brain works.

**Humor register:** Deadpan primary, sardonic secondary, absurdist occasional. Matches the project's humor profile. K is never self-deprecating — he's too earnest for that at 21 and too amused for that post-return.

---

## Voice 1: Narrator-K (Audio, 1989-1993)

### Who He Is at This Age

A 21-year-old Order employee who genuinely believes in the mission. Technically gifted — the Order gave him the narration assignment because nobody senior wanted it and K volunteered because he's passionate. He rehearses his lines. He's slightly nervous. He reads the institutional script like it matters.

### How He Sounds

- **Sentence structure:** Formal, complete sentences. No fragments. Subordinate clauses. "The tablets, discovered in a sealed chamber adjacent to the famous cave paintings, represent..."
- **Vocabulary:** Institutional academic. "Designated," "catalogued," "hypothesized," "systematic." He's performing expertise.
- **Rhythm:** Even, measured. Museum audio guide cadence. Each sentence lands before the next begins.
- **Emotion:** Leaks around the edges, never in the content. K doesn't say "this is beautiful." K says "Dr. Marchand described the chamber as having an almost ceremonial quality" — letting someone else's words carry the awe.
- **Humor:** Unintentional. K at 21 doesn't know he's funny. The humor comes from how seriously he takes absurd material.

### Vocal Stage Differences

**1989-1990 (Cave wing, early exhibits):**
- Most rehearsed. Careful enunciation.
- Reads exactly what's on the page.
- Occasional over-emphasis on words he finds important — a tiny tell that he cares about the material more than an employee should.
- No improvisation. The script IS the performance.

**1990-1992 (Egyptian, Classical, Medieval wings):**
- Looser. More natural delivery.
- Small asides that go slightly off-script: "Note the systematic use of placement markers and the clear indication of rotational direction" — the "clear indication" is K editorializing, not quoting the script.
- Admiration for the material leaking through. A half-second pause before a particularly beautiful exhibit description, as if he just looked at it again.
- Still institutional register, but less robotic.

**1992-1993 (Later wings, last recordings):**
- Tighter. MORE professional, not less.
- Fewer asides. K has pulled back to the script because off-script moments feel dangerous.
- Micro-hesitations that could be read as careful delivery or as nervousness.
- Sounds like a better narrator, which is actually the sound of someone hiding.
- The last recordings are from just before the Bellweather. On second playthrough: that's when K stopped being himself on tape.

### What He Would Never Say

- Anything casual or colloquial ("pretty cool," "check this out")
- First person singular ("I think," "I believe") — always institutional "we" or passive voice
- Direct emotional statements ("this is beautiful," "this is frightening")
- Anything that acknowledges the absurdity of the content
- Contractions (at least in the early recordings; maybe one or two slip in by 1992)

### Sample Narrator Lines by Stage

**1989 (Cave wing):**
> "The sequence depicted — now catalogued as OOGA-1 in the Proto-Kinetic Index — shows a four-beat pattern using what appear to be burning branches. Analysis of wear patterns suggests these tablets were handled frequently, likely for instructional purposes."

**1991 (Egyptian wing):**
> "The burial chamber contained forty-seven distinct placement diagrams. Forty-seven. Each one a complete notation. The pharaoh was, it seems, quite thorough in her documentation."

(Note the repetition of "forty-seven" — K going slightly off-script, emphasizing what strikes him.)

**1993 (Modern wing, late recording):**
> "Protocol Lethe is initiated when containment breach reaches Classification Level Seven. The procedure follows standard departmental channels. Three requisitions are submitted simultaneously to Media Degaussing, Cognitive Reclassification, and Digital Archive Redaction. Processing time varies."

(Flat. Professional. No editorializing. K has gone cold on tape.)

## Voice 2: Annotator-K (Written, post-2008)

### Who He Is Now

The same person, decades later, returned to the building he once worked in. He's processing rooms he helped build, listening to his own voice from twenty years ago, and writing responses. He knows exactly how absurd this all is. He's warm without being sentimental. His humor is the humor of survival.

### How He Writes

- **Sentence structure:** Short. Fragments. Sometimes just a word. The compression of someone who's been thinking about this for decades and no longer needs to explain.
- **Vocabulary:** Plain English. No institutional language. "Looks like scratches. It's a lesson plan." — where narrator-K would need 40 words, annotator-K needs 8.
- **Rhythm:** Staccato. Punchy. Occasional long sentence when something genuinely moves him.
- **Emotion:** Present and unguarded. K isn't performing anymore. "This is my favorite room" is a statement of fact, not a confession.
- **Humor:** Intentional, warm, deadpan. The amusement of someone who understands the full context. Never cruel. Never at anyone's expense except the institution's.

### Annotation Evolution

**Early return (first months):**
- Sharpie scribbles in margins of existing plaques
- Hasty, sometimes barely legible
- Reactive: "wrong" / "they knew" / "ask about the filing date"
- Processing mode — K is going through rooms he helped build and having visceral responses
- Not yet writing for an audience. Writing for himself.

**Settling in (months 2-6):**
- More deliberate placement. Choosing where to annotate.
- Starting to address the visitor: "look closer" / "the date matters"
- Corrective: actively reframing the Order's clinical descriptions
- The handwriting is neater. K is slowing down.

**Established (6+ months):**
- Proper signs alongside or replacing sticky notes
- Writing for visitors now, not for himself
- Warm, inviting, occasionally funny
- "This is my favorite room" / "Do not attempt to replicate what you see. (yes, do.)"
- The visual quality of the signs is noticeably better. K has found art supplies.

**Late period (years in):**
- A few annotations in different handwriting — converted Order members who joined K
- K's own annotations are confident, crafted, sometimes poetic in a compressed way
- The closing annotation lives here

### What He Would Never Write

- Anything in institutional register ("it should be noted," "per departmental guidelines")
- Long explanations. If he needs more than 30 words, he's overthinking it.
- Anything bitter. K is past bitterness. He's amused now.
- Direct attacks on the Order. The annotations are corrections, not vengeance.
- His own name or identity. The annotations are unsigned.

### Sample Annotations by Stage

**Early return:**
> wrong.

(Written in thick sharpie across an Order plaque claiming the Edo practitioner's work was "unclassifiable." That's all. One word.)

**Settling in:**
> She had a name. They filed it.

(Next to the empty Edo folder.)

**Established:**
> Do not attempt to replicate what you see.
> *(yes, do.)*

(Below an Order safety warning. The italicized second line is smaller, warmer.)

**Late period:**
> The exhibits are closing. Go make something.

(Candidate closing annotation. Fourth-wall transparency without fourth-wall break.)

## Voice 3: The Order (Written Documents)

### What the Order Sounds Like

Bureaucratic. Clinical. Procedural. The Order is a government department that has existed for millennia and sounds like one. Authority without understanding. Triplicate forms, approval chains, institutional inertia. Not evil, just incredibly governmental.

### How Order Documents Read

- **Sentence structure:** Passive voice. Nominalization. "The containment of kinetic materials requires..." not "We contain kinetic materials."
- **Vocabulary:** Bureaucratese. "Pursuant to," "in accordance with," "per standard operating procedure," "recommend reclassification." Filing numbers on everything.
- **Tone:** Dead serious. The content is absurd (quarantine procedures for people who spin sticks) but the presentation never acknowledges this.
- **Comedy mechanism:** The gap between the gravity of the language and the triviality of the subject. A 150-word requisition form requesting budget to suppress a notation system used by twelve people.

### Document Types

**Plaques:** Formal museum labels. Clinical descriptions of exhibits. "Discovered in," "believed to be," "scholars have noted." No opinion, just documentation.

**Memos:** Internal communication. "TO: Division Head, Western Hemisphere. RE: Unauthorized kinetic notation distribution, Pacific Northwest." Banality of bureaucratic evil.

**Requisition forms:** Budget requests. "Requisition 7741-B: Suppress kinetic notation in Pacific Northwest. Estimated cost: $12,400. Justification: See attached field report." The specificity of the dollar amounts is part of the comedy.

**Meeting transcripts:** Multiple speakers. Formal minutes format. Maximum dramatic irony on second playthrough because K is in these meetings suggesting solutions that make things worse.

**Field reports:** Agent observations. "Subject observed engaging in sustained kinetic activity (est. 3 hours). Recommend continued monitoring. Subject appears to be experiencing temporal dissociation consistent with Stage 2 exposure."

**Safety warnings:** "DO NOT ATTEMPT TO REPLICATE MOVEMENTS DEPICTED IN THIS EXHIBIT. Exposure risk: MODERATE."

### What the Order Would Never Write

- Anything that acknowledges the absurdity of their mission
- Anything warm or human
- First person ("I saw," "I think") — always institutional voice
- Casual language of any kind
- Humor (they are 100% sincere, always)

### Sample Order Documents

**Memo:**
> TO: Regional Director, European Operations
> FROM: Nomenclature Division
> RE: Designation Anomaly — Proto-Kinetic Sequence OOGA-1
>
> During routine cataloguing of the Lascaux notation fragments (ref. PKI-1979-0043), the assigned Latin-letter designation produced an unexpected phonetic result. The four-beat sequence, when rendered through the standard conversion protocol, yields the string "OOGA."
>
> We are requesting guidance on whether this is coincidental or warrants reclassification under Protocol 12-C (Linguistic Anomaly, Pre-Historic).
>
> Note: The Nomenclature Division wishes to state for the record that the conversion protocol was applied correctly and without editorial intervention.

**Field report:**
> FIELD OBSERVATION — CASE 1993-NW-0447
>
> Subject: Unidentified female, est. age 25-30
> Location: Public park, Portland, OR
> Duration of observation: 47 minutes
>
> Subject engaged in sustained bilateral kinetic activity using two cylindrical implements (classification: STAVES). Movements consistent with Type 2 and Type 3 patterns documented in the Lascaux and Cairo materials.
>
> Subject displayed classic Stage 2 exposure symptoms: temporal dissociation (checked watch once in 47 minutes), repetitive motion refinement, and apparent unawareness of observer presence.
>
> Recommend: Continued monitoring. Do not engage.
>
> Note: Subject was smiling.

(The last line — "Note: Subject was smiling" — is the field agent going slightly off-protocol. The smile isn't a symptom. The agent noticed anyway. This is how the Order's humanity leaks through despite the system.)

## Cross-Voice Interactions

The magic of the museum is moments where the voices collide.

**Narrator says something; annotator responds:**
> NARRATOR (audio): "Do not attempt to replicate what you see. These movements, while visually compelling, represent a significant exposure risk."
>
> ANNOTATOR (sticky note below the speaker): "yes, do."

**Order document with K's marginalia:**
> ORDER MEMO: "Requisition 7741-B: Suppress kinetic notation in Pacific Northwest."
>
> K'S SHARPIE IN MARGIN: "denied. — K"

(This one only works on second playthrough. First time through, the player doesn't know who K is or why they'd write "denied" on an old form. Second time through: K is the person who made sure this requisition never went through.)

**Meeting transcript where K is present:**
> CHAIR: "The bottleneck in processing has caused a 340% increase in approval wait times."
> K: "Have we considered adding a secondary review stage? That might catch errors earlier in the pipeline."
> CHAIR: "Excellent suggestion. Implement immediately."

(First playthrough: competent employee. Second playthrough: K just added another layer of bureaucratic paralysis on purpose.)
